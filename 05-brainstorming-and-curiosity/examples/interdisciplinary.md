# 💡 Brainstorming Examples

[⬅ Back to Brainstorming & Curiosity](../README.md)

Two examples: a general outline you can use on any idea, then a practical example from computer science. The AI responses are illustrative. Your own chat will differ, and that's fine.

---

## Part 1: The general outline

Use these five steps on any idea, in any field. Swap the brackets for your own words.

### Step 1: Your idea

- Write a rough idea in your own words **before** opening AI.
- It can be messy. A sentence or two is enough.
- **Why it matters:** if AI gives you the first idea, you start by reacting to it instead of thinking for yourself.

### Step 2: Directions

- **Prompt:** "Here's my idea: [idea]. Give me several different directions I could take. Don't pick one."
- **Look for:** approaches you hadn't considered, and at least one that surprises you.
- **Try next:** "Give me three more that are very different from these."

### Step 3: Assumptions and gaps

- **Prompt:** "What assumptions am I making? What risks and missing perspectives should I consider?"
- **Look for:** things you took for granted, and people the idea would affect who you hadn't thought about.
- **Try next:** "Who would disagree with this, and why?"

### Step 4: The strongest case against

- **Prompt:** "Make the strongest case against this idea."
- **Look for:** the one objection that is hard to answer. That's the one worth examining.
- **Try next:** "What would make this idea fail?" and "What evidence would change my mind?"

### Step 5: Your decision

- Decide what to **keep**, **change**, **test**, or **discard**.
- Use your own criteria: **relevance, feasibility, evidence, cost, and who may be affected**.
- This step is yours. AI can help you list options, but it shouldn't make the call.

### Common mistakes

- Asking AI to pick the best idea for you.
- Stopping at the first set of directions.
- Treating the case against your idea as a verdict instead of another angle.
- Skipping step 5 and keeping everything.

### The outline in action: a study group

**1. My idea**
"I want to start a weekly study group for my class."

**2. Directions**
- A drop-in group anyone can join.
- A small, fixed group that commits for the semester.
- A shared online notes page with no meetings at all.

**3. Assumptions and gaps**
- I assumed everyone has the same free time.
- Commuters and students with jobs may not be able to attend.
- Quieter students may not speak up in a big group.

**4. Strongest case against**
"A big group can turn into a social hour. The students who need help most may be the least likely to join or speak up."

**5. My decision**
- **Keep:** the weekly group.
- **Change:** offer two time slots instead of one.
- **Test:** run it for three weeks before making it official.
- **Discard:** the online notes page, since nobody asked for it.

## Part 2: A practical example from computer science

**Project:** a small web app that helps students find open study rooms on campus.

### Step 1: My idea

"I want to build a web app that shows which study rooms are open right now."

### Step 2: Directions

> Here's my idea: a web app that shows which study rooms are open. Give me several different ways I could build it. Don't pick one.

- **Use the existing booking calendar.** Read room availability from the system the school already uses.
- **Crowdsourced check-ins.** Students tap a button when they take or leave a room.
- **Sensors in the rooms.** Detect whether someone is inside.
- **A simple page updated by hand.** Staff or volunteers post what's open.

### Step 3: Assumptions, risks, and missing perspectives

> What assumptions am I making? What risks and missing perspectives should I consider?

**Assumptions**
- The booking data is available to me. It may not be, or it may need permission.
- Students want to check a web app instead of walking by the room.

**Risks**
- Check-ins only work if enough students use them. Stale data is worse than no data.
- Sensors raise cost and privacy questions.
- Someone has to maintain the app after I graduate.

**Missing perspectives**
- A student who uses a wheelchair or needs a quiet space may need room details my app doesn't show.
- The staff who manage the rooms may have rules I don't know about.

### Step 4: Strongest case against

> Make the strongest case against this idea.

"The school's booking system probably already shows this. Your app may only add a second place to check, and it breaks the moment the data goes out of date."

**Follow-up:** "What evidence would change my mind?"

- If most students say they have no good way to find a room now, the app has a real gap to fill.
- If the booking system already has a clear, mobile-friendly availability page, the app adds little.

### Step 5: What I decide

*Criteria: feasibility, evidence, cost, who may be affected.*

| Decision | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Test** | Ask five students how they find a room now and what annoys them. | Evidence: I'm guessing there's a problem, and this checks it. |
| **Test** | Check whether the booking data is available to me. | Feasibility: no data access, no app. |
| **Keep** | The crowdsourced check-in idea, as a small prototype for one building. | Cost: it's the cheapest option to try. |
| **Change** | Plan for room details, like accessibility and noise level. | Who may be affected: it was a gap I hadn't considered. |
| **Discard** | Sensors. | Cost and privacy: too much for a first version. |

### What happens next

- Interview the five students and write down what they say, in their words.
- Ask staff whether the booking data can be shared.
- If both checks look good, build a rough prototype and test it for a week.
- If not, drop the idea or change it to match what I learned.

---

## What to notice

- The idea always starts with a person, not the AI.
- AI offers directions and challenges. It never makes the final call.
- Each decision is made against your own criteria.
- The case against the idea is another angle to examine, not a verdict. Test it with people and facts outside the chat.
